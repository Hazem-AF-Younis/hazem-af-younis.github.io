I am a PhD candidate in Computer Engineering at The University of Texas at Dallas (May 2027), working on speech processing and machine learning for people with hearing loss in the Cochlear Implant Lab at the Center for Robust Speech Systems. My research asks whether talker familiarity, one of the strongest advantages in speech perception, can be engineered. I use deep-learning voice conversion to make unfamiliar voices sound closer to ones a cochlear implant user already knows, and I test the results with real listeners.

I am also an engineering educator. I teach UT Dallas's first-year engineering course and lead labs in digital systems and circuits, and I especially enjoy teaching digital logic, computer architecture, and embedded systems.

Feel free to reach out about speech technology, engineering education, or collaboration.

Teaching
======
For three fall semesters I have been the instructor of record for UT Dallas's required first-year engineering course, ECS 1100, teaching 525 students in sections that ranged from 11 to 297. In Fall 2025, across two lectures of 493 students, the item "Overall, this instructor was excellent" averaged 4.60 out of 5. As a teaching assistant, I led lab sections in Introduction to Digital Systems and Introduction to ECE II. I hold the UT Dallas Graduate Teaching Certificate and the Engineers Who Teach Professional Badge. [More about my teaching](/teaching/).

Research
======
In the Cochlear Implant Lab at UT Dallas's Center for Robust Speech Systems, I build and test speech processing systems for people with hearing loss. My doctoral work uses deep-learning voice conversion to make unfamiliar talkers sound closer to voices a cochlear implant user already knows, then tests whether that familiarity helps the listener understand them. I also built CCi-CLOUD, a cloud platform for remote listening experiments, as part of the NIH-funded CCi-MOBILE project. [More about my research](/research/).
